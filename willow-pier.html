The Last Light at Willow Pier

Some promises are kept long after everyone has forgotten them.

Page & Pixel · Fiction01 / 02

The Lantern

Every evening at seven, the old lantern at the end of Willow Pier lit itself.

No one had tended the lantern for thirty years. The lighthouse keeper had moved away, the fishing boats had stopped coming, and the wooden boards creaked under the weight of nobody at all. Yet every night, as the clock above the bakery struck seven, a golden light blinked awake over the water.

Most people in the village called it a trick of the sunset. Mara, who had never been very good at believing what everyone else believed, decided to investigate.

She arrived at the pier with a flashlight, a notebook, and a sandwich wrapped in paper. The sea was perfectly still. At the far end, the lantern's glass was fogged, but the flame inside burned steady and bright.

There was no match, no oil, and no wire leading to it. Beneath the lantern sat a small tin box, painted blue like a summer sky. Across the lid, in tiny silver letters, someone had written: For the one who stays until the light goes out.

Mara opened the box. Inside lay a letter, a brass key, and a photograph of two children on the very same pier. One of them held a paper boat. The other held a lantern.

The letter was short: When the river forgets its way to the sea, follow the light home.

Mara looked up. Across the black water, where no island had ever stood, a second light began to glow.

The Promise

It wasn't an island. It was a boat, so small and pale that it looked like a folded piece of moonlight.

The boat drifted toward the pier without a splash. On its bow hung a lock shaped like a star. Mara fitted the brass key into it, and the little door of a wooden chest swung open.

Inside was a second photograph. The two children were older now, standing beneath a banner that read Willow Pier, Opening Day. On the back, someone had written: We promised to keep a light burning until the last boat found its way back.

Mara knew the girl in the picture. It was her grandmother, who had spent her life telling stories about the sea, and who always left a lamp glowing in the kitchen window.

At the bottom of the chest lay a folded map, drawn in blue ink. A thin line wound from the village through the marshes and into the sea. Mara recognized the path: it was the old river, the one that had dried up before she was born.

She raised her flashlight and traced the line across the dark shoreline. Beyond the reeds, she heard something she had never heard there before—the quiet rush of moving water. A stream had returned after the evening rain, and it was filling the riverbed one little bend at a time.

As the first trickle reached the sea, the lantern at Willow Pier flickered. Then it went dark.

Mara waited, expecting the night to feel empty. Instead, across the village, one kitchen window shone gold. Then another. Then a third. The people of Willow had noticed the water and come out with lamps of their own.

She put the photographs carefully into her notebook and walked home beneath a hundred small lights. In the morning, she would ask her grandmother about the promise. Tonight, it was enough to know that someone had remembered it.
